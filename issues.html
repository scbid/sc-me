What We Are Fighting

Closing In from Both Ends

Two separate developers. Two separate projects. One community caught in the middle, being taxed for its own displacement.

North end, Project 1

The N. Saddle Creek BID

An unaccountable Business Improvement District run by a developer from Bennington. Mandatory assessments, no transparency, no community voice. Local businesses are legally required to fund it.

⟷
South end, Project 2 — Urgent

The Family Fare Project

A developer seeking tax incentives to demolish seven immigrant-owned businesses and replace them with market-rate apartments. Final hearing June 2. Construction could begin immediately after.

The two projects are geographically separate but structurally connected. Together they are squeezing Saddle Creek from both ends of N Saddle Creek Road.

01

The Family Fare Project

Final hearing June 2. Once construction begins, these businesses are gone permanently.

Most Urgent

The Family Fare lot at the south end of the Saddle Creek corridor is at the center of the Saddle Creek Marketplace proposal: 223 luxury apartments and 67,000 square feet of retail, developed by Mitch Hohlen, who is seeking $9 million in public Tax Increment Financing and an Enhanced Employment Area designation. The total project cost is $110 million. There is no affordable housing in the proposal.

The developer is not paying for this out of pocket. He is seeking public tax incentives, meaning the Omaha community subsidizes the destruction of businesses that have served this neighborhood for years. The people being displaced help fund the mechanism of their own displacement. One affordable housing unit has already been lost because of this project, a concrete example of the displacement that is already underway.

Businesses and organizations at risk

These are real places that real people depend on, including immigrants, refugees, and neighbors who rely on them for cultural foods, essential services, and community connection.

Saddle Creek Plaza businesses facing demolition

  • Family Fare
  • Tawakal African Foods
  • China Buffet
  • Gyro Kings
  • Hello Nepal
  • Lee Beauty Supply
  • Papa John's
  • Omaha Center for Refugee and Immigrant Services

These businesses provide groceries, cultural foods, employment, and essential refugee and immigrant services to communities that have few alternatives nearby. Global Foods in the N. Saddle Creek BID corridor plays a similar role for that end of the street. Losing any of them is not a minor inconvenience. For many neighbors it means losing access to food, to services, and to institutions that were built specifically to serve them.

These businesses were not consulted. They were not offered real alternatives or relocation assistance. They were told they would need to vacate so that a developer could capture public subsidies to build housing that most of their customers cannot afford.

What the tax incentives mean

Tax increment financing and similar tools are intended to spur development in genuinely distressed areas. When applied here, they redirect future tax revenue away from public services, schools, and infrastructure, and toward subsidizing a private development that the community did not ask for and that does not serve existing residents.

In plain terms: this community is being asked to fund a project that destroys its own businesses and prices out its own residents while generating returns for a private developer. The community bears the cost. One person captures the gain. We will not pay for our own displacement.

What is being lost

Seven businesses that have operated in Saddle Creek for years, in some cases for decades. Immigrant-owned, community-rooted, serving neighbors who have relied on them. These are not marginal operations. They are part of the fabric of this corridor. Once demolished, they will not come back. The neighborhood that replaces them will not be the same one.

This is a HUD-designated food desert. Parts of our neighborhood are designated as a food desert by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. Family Fare is one of the only grocery stores serving this community. Tawakal African Foods, China Buffet, and Gyro Kings provide cultural foods that many neighbors cannot access elsewhere. The strip mall also houses an immigrant and refugee service provider that helps community members navigate essential services. Demolishing all of this does not solve the food access problem. It makes it catastrophically worse.

Our position: We oppose this proposal in its current form. No public tax incentives should subsidize the demolition of functioning community businesses in a food desert. We support intentional, community-led development that solves real problems rather than creating new ones. We are calling on every city council member to vote NO on June 2, and on the mayor's office to intervene before construction begins.

What is being voted on

The proposal involves Tax Increment Financing (TIF) and an Enhanced Employment Area (EEA) designation, both forms of public tax subsidy being sought by the developer. These are new taxes on the area that redirect public revenue away from schools and services and toward subsidizing a private development project. The community did not ask for them. The community was not consulted about them.

The hearing schedule

There have been two public hearings already, on May 19 and May 27. The final public hearing is June 2. After that, a council vote. After that, construction can begin. There is very little time.

02

The N. Saddle Creek BID

An existing Business Improvement District that has operated without community input since its creation.

The N. Saddle Creek Business Improvement District was created without meaningful input from the longstanding local businesses and residents it claims to serve. It is run by a developer who lives in Bennington, not in Saddle Creek, and who stands to gain financially from the decisions the BID makes.

His wife, his tenant, and one other absentee property owner complete the leadership. There is no authentic local representation. The businesses legally required to fund the BID through mandatory assessments have no real voice in how their money is spent.

What the BID has done

  • Spent mandatory assessment funds with no transparent public accounting
  • Selected contractors without any disclosed competitive process
  • Held meetings at a storage unit facility two miles from the neighborhood it claims to represent
  • Required BID funds to pay for private security, addressing problems created by the same developer's own deliberately neglected properties
  • Closed a public access road at the developer's request, over explicit community objection, and is now attempting to replat and sell that public land as a private lot
  • Advanced city ordinances while the mayor was out of town, signed by a council president who introduced them himself

Businesses and residents opposing the BID

The N. Saddle Creek BID is also attempting to displace several locally owned family businesses in the north corridor. These businesses and their neighbors are united in opposition.

Taxed for our own displacement

The BID collects mandatory assessments from local businesses. Those funds have been used in ways that benefit the developer who controls the BID, not the businesses paying into it. Merchants are legally required to fund an organization that works against their interests. That is the definition of being taxed for your own displacement.

TIF and deliberate blight

The BID developer also owns multiple vacant and deteriorated lots in the corridor. That vacancy and deterioration helps qualify the area for Tax Increment Financing, a public subsidy he then receives. He is deliberately holding properties in disrepair in order to manufacture the conditions that justify public money flowing to him. The neighborhood pays the cost in blight and lost activity. He captures the benefit.

City council accountability

Multiple ordinances affecting this corridor were introduced by Council Member Pete Festersen and advanced while the mayor was out of town, meaning they were signed off by the council president rather than subject to normal mayoral review. The council president at the time was Festersen himself. His successor, Danny Begley, has continued to approve decisions despite documented neighborhood opposition.

Our coalition met directly with Festersen and Begley before the TIF and EEA votes. They told us they would try to include affordable housing units, food access requirements, and business relocation support in the TIF agreement. None of it appeared in the final agreement. What did appear was a new provision preventing renters in the affected area from contesting property tax increases, a protection stripped from the most vulnerable residents and written into a public subsidy deal for a luxury developer. They did not try. They made it worse.

Our position: The N. Saddle Creek BID should be dissolved. Any future district must have genuine community governance, transparent finances, and leadership that actually lives and works in Saddle Creek.

03

Why These Projects Are Connected

These are two separate developers and two separate proposals. But they share a structure: public money, tax incentives, and mandatory assessments are being used to subsidize private development that displaces the existing community rather than serving it.

At the north end, a developer controls a BID, collects assessments from local businesses, and uses the resulting influence and public tools to advance his own interests. The businesses he was supposed to support have seen their streets disrupted and their assessments spent without accountability.

At the south end, a different developer proposes to use tax incentives to demolish seven immigrant-owned businesses for market-rate apartments. The Family Fare lot, a functioning part of the Saddle Creek corridor, would be razed. The businesses that operated there would be gone. The housing that replaces them will not serve the people who lived and worked in this neighborhood.

Together, these two projects are closing in on Saddle Creek from both ends. The corridor is being remade, not for the people who built it, but for the people who see profit in it.

This is not just about working people

It would be easy to frame this as a fight for low-income residents or struggling small businesses. That framing is too narrow. What is happening in Saddle Creek affects everyone who shops here, eats here, relies on these businesses, walks these streets.

We believe every neighbor deserves access to opportunity: cultural foods that reflect their heritage, job training, employment, refugee and immigrant services, and the local institutions that make a neighborhood function. When the Omaha Center for Refugee and Immigrant Services is demolished, that is not a small loss. When Tawakal African Foods and Hello Nepal close, neighbors lose access to food that matters to them. These are not replaceable by a market-rate apartment building.

The loss of affordable housing is a loss for the whole city. The corruption of public tax tools into private subsidy machines is a loss for every taxpayer. This is everyone's fight.

What we want instead

Saddle Creek runs deep. The families, the businesses, and the institutions that have been part of this corridor for generations are not obstacles to progress. They are the neighborhood. We are not against change or investment. We are against the specific kind of change that extracts value from this community and delivers it to people who do not live here. We will not pay for our own displacement. We want development that serves the people already in Saddle Creek. We want public money used for public benefit. We want the community at the table before decisions are made, not informed of them afterward.

June 2 is the deadline.
